№ 01 — The work

An instrument that does not need a player.

Vox Arboris is a tree, cut from sheet and standing on its own floor, with a complete modular synthesizer built into its trunk. Two banks of electronics sit at playing height on either side of the console. Patch cables fall from the branches like aerial roots. Switch it on and it begins to play — slowly, deliberately, never quite repeating — and it keeps playing whether or not anyone is standing at it.

It has spent four years in galleries and clubs: a month-long residency at Ten Club, a month at WIHH, a night at Odonien in Cologne, and a set high above the IJ at the A'dam Tower. It is now looking for a permanent home.

№ 02 — What it does

Three things, and it does them at once.

01

It plays itself

The system is patched as a self-generating instrument. Clock, chance and slow modulation drive the voices; nothing is sequenced by hand and nothing loops back to the same bar. Left running in a room it produces a continuous evolving score for as long as it is switched on — a piece of music that is different every day and recognisably itself every time.

02

It listens to a plant

A living plant is wired into the circuit. Electrodes read the minute fluctuations in its surface conductivity — the plant's response to light, water, touch, the room — and a biodata module turns that signal into pitch and movement. A harmonic quantiser catches it and places it in key, so what you hear is not noise from a plant but the plant playing in tune.

It is not a metaphor and not a recording. Water the plant and the music changes.

03

It draws sound in fire

A metre-long Rubens' tube runs out along one branch: a sealed pipe of gas with a row of jets down its length and a driver at one end. Standing waves inside vary the pressure along the tube, so the flames stand tall where the pressure is high and low where it isn't. The row of flames becomes a picture of the sound, redrawing itself as the music moves.

Heinrich Rubens demonstrated the principle in 1905. It has lost none of its effect on a room.

№ 03 — The design

Where the sequencer was, there is now a plant.

For four years this instrument was driven by a rack of drum machines and samplers sitting at the centre of the console — the brain, flanked by two banks of modular.

In its acquired form that centre bay is empty of electronics. The two electronic wings remain either side, and the plant takes the middle: the thing that now decides what happens sits exactly where the machine that used to decide it was bolted down. The instrument was rebuilt around that substitution. Nothing in it depends on a laptop, a phone, or a sequencer you have to learn.

The plant is not an input to the instrument. It is the part of the instrument that chooses.

№ 06 — What is acquired

The whole instrument, installed and working.

This is not a crate of parts. It arrives assembled, patched, tuned and playing, with everything needed to keep it that way.

The sculpture
Cut tree form with integrated console and sculpted floor element. Freestanding. Approx. 2 m tall, footprint approx. 2 × 1.5 m.
The instrument
Two Eurorack banks flanking the plant bay: percussion voices, melodic and textural voices, granular and reverb processing, clock and chance generators, quantisers, mixing and balanced output. Full module inventory supplied on request and in the manual.
Plant interface
Biodata module and electrodes. A second plant channel can be added on request.
Fire
One-metre Rubens' tube with driver and mounting. Runs on bottled propane, user-operated.
Documentation
A printed manual — module inventory, signal path, per-module patch breakdowns explaining every connection and why it is made, the default patches with recall notes, and troubleshooting. Written so that someone who has never touched a modular system can switch it on, understand what they are hearing, and rebuild the patch from nothing if it is ever pulled apart. Two copies: one for you, one that lives with the work.
Installation
Delivered and installed on site in person, with a walkthrough for the people who will look after it.
Tuning
Two sessions included. The final patch is developed with you — tempo, density, tonality, how strongly the plant drives it, how the fire is used. A laboratory foyer wants something different from a club.

A venue's real question is not what it costs. It is what happens when it stops working and nobody here knows modular. That is what the manual is for.
